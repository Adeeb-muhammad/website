WILL TODAY'S BEST AI BE FREE IN 2036?

A decade from now, will the most capable AI models still be expensive—or will today's premium intelligence become something almost anyone can use?

By Adeeb Muhammad14 November 20265 min read

Imagine opening an AI assistant in 2036 and getting help with a complicated software project, a research question, or a creative idea without thinking about the cost of every message. Would that be normal?

I think it is possible. Not guaranteed, and probably not unlimited—but possible.

01 / The price of intelligence

Frontier AI is not free to operate. Companies pay for specialised chips, electricity, data centres, engineering, and the work needed to train and serve increasingly capable models. Those costs show up in API prices and subscription plans.

For a real reference point, Anthropic lists Claude Opus 5.5 at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens on its API pricing page. That is a current price, not a prediction about the future.

$4 / $20

USD per million tokens — input / output pricing for Claude Opus 5.5, as listed in November 2026.

A token is a small unit of text processed by a model. Input and output are priced separately.

02 / My 2036 price guess

If hardware becomes more efficient, models get better at using fewer computations, and competition keeps increasing, the cost of accessing very capable AI could fall dramatically.

My deliberately rough guess for a frontier-class model in 2036 is:

$0.10–$1

Per million input tokens

$0.50–$5

Per million output tokens

Speculative ranges in 2036 USD—not a forecast from Anthropic or any other company.

There are several reasons this could happen:

  • More efficient hardware: future chips may deliver more useful computation per watt and per dollar.
  • Better model design: smarter architectures may achieve similar results with less computation.
  • Competition: more capable open models and competing providers could push prices down.
  • Smaller models: techniques such as distillation and quantisation could make strong capabilities cheaper to serve.

But there is a counterargument: companies may keep building much more capable systems, and the very best models may remain expensive because demand for them will be high. Lower costs do not automatically mean every premium feature becomes free.

03 / Could today's top ChatGPT intelligence become free?

I think a model with capabilities comparable to today's premium reasoning systems could eventually be available on a free tier. That is different from saying today's exact premium model will become free.

Free access could come with limits: fewer messages, slower responses at busy times, smaller context windows, or restricted access to expensive tools. A free tier can still be genuinely useful without offering unlimited access to the most expensive system.

So, could something at today's “Astra-level” capability become free one day? I would not rule it out. But I would not treat it as a promise either. Product names, model tiers, and plan limits will change many times before 2036.

04 / What I think 2036 looks like

CapabilityMy expectation
Today's premium-level intelligenceMuch more widely accessible
Advanced coding assistantsCommon, with useful free tiers
Personal AI assistantsNormal everyday tools
Long-running autonomous agentsMore capable, but likely metered
The absolute best frontier modelsPossibly still paid or usage-limited

The biggest change may not be that the best AI costs nothing. It may be that the level of intelligence we currently pay a premium for becomes ordinary—and the frontier moves even further ahead.

05 / The part that excites me

Think about how many tools that once required expensive computers are now available on ordinary phones and laptops. AI could follow a similar path, even if the economics are more complicated.

In 2036, I suspect the question will be less about whether someone can access a capable AI and more about how much autonomy, reliability, speed, and computing power they get for free.

And maybe we'll look back at today's limits—small local models, message caps, and expensive frontier APIs—and find them surprisingly restrictive.
